\chapter{Experimental Results}
\label{chap6}

This chapter presents the results of the automated evaluation across all six configurations. It first describes the evaluation setup, then reports the headline scores and analyses them from several angles: retrieval quality, generation quality, the handling of unanswerable questions, the separate effects of the chunking strategy and the language model, response latency, and token consumption. It closes with qualitative observations on recurring answer patterns and a no-retrieval baseline that isolates the contribution of the retrieval component.

\section{Evaluation Setup}

All six configurations were evaluated using GPT-4o-mini as the external judge for both RAGAS metrics and the LLM-as-a-Judge scores. Each configuration was run on the same stratified 20-question sample described in Section~\ref{sec:eval-sampling}. Fourteen of the twenty questions were answerable (is\_impossible=False) and six were unanswerable. RAGAS metrics were computed only on the answerable subset. Judge Correctness was also restricted to answerable questions. Judge Conciseness was computed for all non-error answers. Results for each question were written to a timestamped CSV file and averaged per configuration. The six unanswerable questions were evaluated manually: for each one, the model's response was inspected to determine whether it correctly acknowledged that the requested information was not present in the contract. A response was counted as correct if it explicitly stated that the answer could not be found, without fabricating a plausible-sounding but unsupported answer. The retriever was configured with $k=10$ chunks per query for all runs. Each configuration was evaluated independently, with no shared conversation history between questions.

Llama 3.1 8B outperforms Mistral 7B on the generation quality metrics in every chunking configuration, but the margin is much narrower than the raw parameter counts would suggest. Averaged across the three chunking strategies, the gap is approximately 0.16 points on Faithfulness (Llama 0.91 vs Mistral 0.75), 0.10 points on Judge Correctness (0.77 vs 0.67), and 0.07 points on Answer Relevancy (0.64 vs 0.57). Semantic chunking carries a clear latency penalty for Llama. Fixed and recursive configurations run in 10-15 seconds per query, whereas Semantic+Llama averages 65 seconds, roughly four to six times longer, with the slowest individual queries on the largest contracts exceeding 300 seconds. Semantic chunks are larger on average than fixed or recursive ones, and generation time scales with context length. Semantic+Llama is therefore awkward for an interactive setting: although its average response time is around a minute, the worst cases run for several minutes, which users would not tolerate in a live chat. It remains viable for batch processing, such as bulk contract analysis. For the real-time chat interface, Recursive+Llama offers the best balance of quality and speed, at roughly 10 seconds per query while retaining the highest Faithfulness of any configuration.

Input token counts are governed by the chunking strategy rather than the model. Recursive configurations are the smallest at roughly 2{,}500 to 3{,}000 tokens per query, fixed configurations pass roughly 4{,}200 to 4{,}900, while semantic chunking passes 8{,}300 to 9{,}600, two to three times as many. A semantic chunk groups a whole topical section, so ten of them carry far more text than ten fixed-size or recursive chunks. This is the same factor behind semantic chunking's higher latency: a longer input takes the model longer to process.

Output token counts divide along model lines. Llama generates roughly 190 to 210 tokens per answer against Mistral's 110 to 150, around one and a half times as many. This is the quantitative form of the difference described in the qualitative observations: Llama tends to quote supporting text more fully, whereas Mistral produces shorter, more direct answers. The gap holds across all three chunking strategies, so it reflects the model rather than the retrieved input.

The input counts also differ slightly between the two models on identical retrieved text, for example 4{,}183 against 4{,}882 tokens for fixed chunking, because Llama and Mistral tokenize the same passage into a different number of tokens. Cross-model comparisons of token counts should therefore be read as approximate rather than exact.

\subsection{Qualitative Observations}

A recurring pattern in Llama's outputs is the inclusion of the retrieved excerpt header and surrounding chunk text in the answer. Even with the prompt instruction to quote only the minimal span that answers the question, Llama frequently emits the source filename in square brackets, followed by adjacent contract text, before the relevant clause. This behaviour explains the Answer Relevancy gap between Llama's fixed and recursive configurations on the one hand and its semantic configuration on the other: with fixed and recursive chunks the answer is correct but verbose, and the verbosity dilutes the relevancy signal. Semantic chunks contain less unrelated material to copy, which is consistent with Llama's highest Answer Relevancy under semantic chunking (0.70). Mistral exhibits the same tendency to a much lesser extent and produces shorter answers on average, which is reflected in its lower output token counts in Table~\ref{tab:results}.

Faithfulness and correctness can diverge when retrieval returns an adjacent passage rather than the target clause. The model produces an answer grounded in what it was given, with no fabrication relative to the retrieved context, but factually wrong relative to the ground truth. Faithfulness on those responses stayed above 0.85 across all six configurations, while judge correctness fell to 0.25. The case shows that faithfulness alone is insufficient as a quality measure: a system can be perfectly faithful to the wrong context.

%The NETGEAR distributor agreement illustrates this: the question on Cap On Liability returned an adjacent indemnification clause rather than the maximum-liability clause in the same section, and every configuration quoted the indemnification text as the answer.

Judge conciseness scored the same correct refusal differently depending on the model's style. When a configuration produced \textit{``Not found: this contract does not appear to contain a [Category] clause''} on its own, the judge scored 1.0 conciseness. When the same refusal was preceded by the retrieved excerpt and a short explanation, as in most of Llama's correct refusals, the same correct outcome scored 0.5 to 0.75. Mistral's refusals were short and direct in nearly every case, which raised its conciseness on the unanswerable set. Llama's habit of citing the excerpt before refusing produced the opposite effect even when the conclusion itself was correct. This is a limitation of applying a general-purpose conciseness measure to a system that surfaces source evidence alongside its conclusions.

\section{Unanswerable Questions}

Six of the twenty questions correspond to clauses that are not present in the queried contracts (\texttt{is\_impossible=True}). Because there is no ground-truth answer text to compare against, these questions were excluded from the automated RAGAS and Judge Correctness calculations. Judge Conciseness was still computed automatically, but conciseness alone does not measure whether the model correctly declined to answer because a short hallucination sometimes scores as well as a short correct refusal.

The six unanswerable questions were therefore evaluated manually. A response was coun\-ted as correct if it ultimately acknowledged that the requested clause was not present in the contract, even if it first discussed adjacent or topically related sections. This rule reflects how the system would be used in practice: surfacing nearby material for a human reviewer to consider is preferable to silently omitting it, provided the model does not falsely claim that the requested clause is present. A response was counted as incorrect if it treated an adjacent clause as the answer or if it concluded that the requested clause might exist when in fact it does not.

All six failures came from Llama configurations; Mistral declined correctly on every unanswerable question in all three chunking strategies. The Llama failures share a common pattern: the retrieved excerpts contained legally adjacent material, an assignment clause for a Third Party Beneficiary question, or an indemnification clause for an Uncapped Liability question, and the model quoted or discussed that material as if it were responsive rather than concluding that the specific clause was absent. The effect is strongest under semantic chunking, where the larger topical passages give the model more adjacent text to latch onto: Semantic+Llama alone accounted for three of the six failures.

This is the flip side of the answering style that helped on answerable questions. Mistral's shorter, more cautious responses worked in its favour here, since a brief refusal is harder to dilute with adjacent clauses than a longer extractive answer. Llama's more elaborate, commit-to-an-answer style, which raised its Faithfulness and Answer Relevancy on answerable questions, made it more prone to treating a partially related clause as the answer when the correct response was that the clause was absent. The two categories that produced almost all of the errors, Third Party Beneficiary and Uncapped Liability, are precisely those whose absence is hardest to confirm, because assignment and indemnification language is superficially similar to the requested provisions. The result is a modest trade-off: the configuration choices that maximise answer quality on answerable questions are also those most likely to over-answer when the clause is genuinely absent.

\section{Comparison with Related Work}

No prior study evaluates a retrieval-augmented pipeline on CUAD with the exact combination of metrics used in this thesis, four RAGAS metrics plus two LLM-as-a-Judge metrics, so a direct comparison against published numbers is not possible. The closest available works each overlap with a different part of this evaluation, and together they place the present results in context.

\textbf{Model comparison.} The ContractEval benchmark \cite{contracteval2025} evaluates fifteen open-source and four proprietary language models on clause-level risk identification over CUAD, using direct prompting rather than retrieval and scoring with F1 and Jaccard similarity. ContractEval reports that larger open-source models do not reliably outperform smaller ones, a 14B model underperforming an 8B variant. This mirrors the present finding that the 7B Mistral is not weaker than the 8B Llama in a way that parameter count alone would explain, and that model behaviour rather than size drives the differences observed. ContractEval also defines a ``laziness'' metric measuring incorrect refusals, the mirror image of the failure mode seen here for Mistral, which occasionally declined an answerable question.

%\textbf{Retrieval architecture.} The study \textit{Towards Reliable Retrieval in RAG Systems for Large Legal Datasets} \cite{reliableretrieval2025} examines retrieval and chunking on CUAD through the LegalBench-RAG benchmark, with tooling close to that used here (recursive character chunking, GPT-4o-mini, and an open-source embedding model). It identifies Document-Level Retrieval Mismatch, where the retriever selects chunks from the wrong source document because legal texts are lexically similar, as the central failure mode of legal RAG, and reports text-level retrieval precision and recall of roughly 0.11 and 0.42 when retrieving across the full corpus. The pipeline in this thesis sidesteps that failure mode by scoping every query to the named contract, which is why its Context Precision (0.91--0.95) and Context Recall (0.87--0.96) are far higher. The comparison is not like-for-like, but it shows that contract-scoped routing removes precisely the bottleneck that work identifies as critical.

\textbf{RAGAS faithfulness.} An empirical evaluation of RAG architectures for policy document question answering \cite{policyrag2026}, which uses the same RAGAS Faithfulness metric over a different, non-legal corpus, reports an average Faithfulness of 0.80 for an advanced RAG configuration against 0.62 for a basic retrieve-then-generate baseline. The best configurations in this thesis reach comparable or higher Faithfulness (0.91-0.93 for Llama), indicating that the hybrid retrieval and contract-scoped routing used here produce grounding at least on par with independently reported advanced-RAG systems, albeit on a different domain.

In summary, where comparison is possible the present system is consistent with the
literature: its model-comparison trends match those of ContractEval and its faithfulness is in line with other RAGAS-evaluated pipelines.

\section{No-Retrieval Baseline}

To quantify the contribution of the retrieval component, both language models were run on the same 20-question sample with no retrieved context. The baseline prompt instructs the model to answer the question from its general legal knowledge alone, or to respond with \textit{``Not found"} if the answer requires reading the specific contract. RAGAS metrics are not applicable in this setting, so the comparison rests on Judge Correctness, Judge Conciseness, and latency.

Table~\ref{tab:baseline} reports the baseline scores alongside the per-model average across the three RAG configurations.

Without any contract text, each model is prompted to answer from general legal knowledge alone. Llama~3.1~8B reaches only 0.36 Judge Correctness in this setting: it either declines (``I am not provided with the contract details") or produces a plausible but generic description of where the requested clause usually appears, neither of which recovers the contract-specific facts the questions ask for. Adding retrieval raises its correctness to 0.77 on average across the three chunking configurations, a gain of roughly 0.41 points. This is the clearest single demonstration of the retrieval component's value: for the stronger generator, more than half of the RAG system's correctness comes from the retrieved context rather than from the model's parametric knowledge.

Mistral~7B shows a much smaller gap, from 0.63 at baseline to 0.67 with retrieval. This narrow margin should not be read as retrieval being unimportant, it reflects two effects. First, Mistral's baseline answers are fluent, well-structured generic legal templates that list the kinds of clauses a lawyer would typically review, and the LLM-as-a-Judge, scoring correctness and completeness, awards such topically appropriate descriptions partial credit even though they contain no facts from the specific contract. The baseline figure of 0.63 therefore overstates how much Mistral actually knows about these contracts. Second, Mistral's RAG correctness is itself only 0.67, below Llama's 0.77, because it draws less effectively on the retrieved context, consistent with its lower Faithfulness in Table~\ref{tab:results}. The small baseline-to-RAG gap is thus a property of the weaker generator, which produces confident ungrounded text and then under-uses the context once it is supplied, not evidence that retrieval is unnecessary.

Conciseness changes little between the two settings (0.73 to 0.75 for Llama, 0.71 to 0.85 for Mistral): the baseline answers are no shorter despite having no excerpts to quote, because both models pad their general-knowledge responses with structured lists. The baseline runs are nonetheless the fastest configurations, since they skip retrieval entirely (Llama 10.5~s, Mistral 7.1~s, below each model's RAG average), confirming that the additional time in the RAG configurations is spent on retrieval and on processing the larger context.
